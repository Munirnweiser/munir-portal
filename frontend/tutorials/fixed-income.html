Fixed Income Essentials

Bonds, the price-yield seesaw, and yield curves — the math behind the biggest asset class on earth.

Key takeaways

Anatomy of a bond

A bond is a loan from the investor to the issuer. Four terms define it: face value (usually 100 or 1,000, repaid at maturity), coupon (the annual interest rate, paid semi-annually or annually), maturity (when principal is repaid), and yield (the market's required return, which moves every day). The coupon is fixed at issuance; the yield is set by the market — and the market price adjusts so that the two reconcile.

Price vs yield: the seesaw

A bond's price is the present value of all its future cash flows, discounted at the yield:

Price = Σ Coupont / (1 + y)t + Face / (1 + y)n

When yields rise, the discount rate rises, and the price falls — the two always move in opposite directions. A bond bought at par (price 100) with a 4% coupon falls below 100 the moment market yields climb above 4%.

Worked example. A 5-year bond, face value 100, 4% annual coupon, market yield 5%:

P = 4/1.05 + 4/1.05² + 4/1.05³ + 4/1.05⁴ + 104/1.05⁵ ≈ 95.67

The bond trades at a discount (95.67 < 100) because its 4% coupon is below the 5% the market demands. If yield fell to 3%, the same math gives ≈ 104.58 — a premium.

Yield curves: the market's forecast

Plotting yields against maturities gives the yield curve. Its shape matters:

  • Normal (upward sloping) — longer maturities pay more; investors expect growth and are compensated for time risk.
  • Inverted — short rates above long rates; historically a reliable recession warning, because the market expects central banks to cut rates later.
  • Flat — transition state; the market is undecided.

Support teams watch curve feeds closely: a missing or stale curve breaks pricing for every bond on the desk, since yields are interpolated from it.

Duration and convexity in one paragraph each

Duration measures price sensitivity: a duration of 7 means a 1% rise in yield drops the price roughly 7%. Longer maturities and lower coupons both increase duration. Convexity is the curvature — the correction term because the price-yield relationship is curved, not straight; it matters most for large yield moves and long-dated bonds.

Credit spreads

Corporate bonds yield more than government bonds of the same maturity. The difference is the credit spread — compensation for default risk. A bond yielding 6% against a 4% government benchmark trades at a 200bp spread. When spreads widen, corporate bond prices fall even if government yields do not move — a frequent source of "why did my bond drop?" tickets.

Accrued interest: who owns the coupon between payments

A bond's coupon accrues every day, but it is only paid on coupon dates. When a bond changes hands between dates, the buyer compensates the seller for the interest earned since the last coupon — this is accrued interest, and the buyer pays the dirty price (clean price + accrued). The day-count convention defines how days are counted; 30/360 (every month = 30 days) is the classic corporate-bond convention.

Worked example (30/360). A bond with 5% annual coupon (face 100) pays semi-annually, so each coupon is 2.50. Last coupon: 15 January. Settlement: 15 May — that is 4 months × 30 = 120 days.

Accrued = 5.00 × 120/360 = 1.67 per 100 face

If the quoted (clean) price is 98.00, the buyer actually pays 98.00 + 1.67 = 99.67. A settlement system booking only the clean price is short 1.67 per 100 — on a 10M trade that is a 16,667 discrepancy, a classic support ticket.

Repo: the plumbing that funds bond desks

A repo (repurchase agreement) is a collateralized short-term loan: the dealer sells bonds today and agrees to buy them back tomorrow (overnight repo) at a slightly higher price. The price difference is the repo rate — the interest on the loan. General collateral (GC) repo accepts any bond from a basket; specials are repos against one specific scarce bond, and their rates can go deeply negative when short-sellers are desperate to borrow it.

Worked example. A dealer repos $10M of bonds overnight at a 5.30% repo rate, with a 2% haircut (the lender's safety margin against price moves).

Cash lent = $10M × (1 − 0.02) = $9.8M
Overnight interest = $9.8M × 0.053 × 1/360 ≈ $1,443

Tomorrow the dealer repays $9,801,443 and gets the bonds back. Support watches: repo fails (bonds not returned), rate spikes into month-end when balance sheets shrink, and collateral eligibility breaks when a bond drops out of the GC basket.

Bond futures and the conversion factor

Bond futures do not deliver one specific bond — the short can deliver any bond from an eligible basket. To make unlike bonds comparable, each carries a conversion factor (CF) that normalizes it to the contract's 6%-coupon notional bond. The invoice price the long pays is:

Invoice = Futures price × CF + accrued interest

The short will always deliver the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) bond — the one minimizing quoted price − futures × CF (the "basis").

Worked example. Futures trade at 120. Bond A: price 118, CF 0.98 → basis = 118 − 120×0.98 = 0.40. Bond B: price 125, CF 1.05 → basis = 125 − 120×1.05 = −1.00. Bond B is CTD (most negative basis). When yields shift, the CTD can switch — and every pricing model, hedge ratio and risk number on the desk changes with it, a favorite source of "the model is wrong" tickets that are really CTD-switch events.

Practice: easy, medium, hard

Try these before moving on — click a question to reveal the answer.

Easy

1. A 5-year 4% annual bond when the market yield is 5% — discount or premium, and why?

Answer: Discount: 95.67 < 100, because its 4% coupon trails the 5% the market demands.

2. Clean price 98.00, accrued 1.67. What does the buyer actually pay?

Answer: The dirty price: 99.67 per 100 of face value.

Medium

3. Compute accrued interest: 5% annual coupon, last coupon 15 January, settlement 15 May (30/360).

Answer: 120 days → 5.00 × 120/360 = 1.67 per 100 face.

4. Futures at 120. Bond A: price 118, CF 0.98 → basis 0.40. Bond B: price 125, CF 1.05 → basis −1.00. Which is CTD?

Answer: Bond B — cheapest-to-deliver is the most negative basis.

Hard

5. A dealer repos $10M of bonds overnight at 5.30% with a 2% haircut. Cash raised and interest?

Answer: Cash = $10M × 0.98 = $9.8M; interest ≈ $9.8M × 0.053 × 1/360 ≈ $1,443.

6. Yields jump and the CTD bond switches. What breaks downstream?

Answer: Pricing models keyed to the old CTD misprice the future; hedges sized on the old DV01 drift; traders complain the futures P&L looks 'wrong'.
