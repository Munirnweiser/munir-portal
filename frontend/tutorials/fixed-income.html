Fixed Income Essentials

Bonds, the price-yield seesaw, and yield curves — the math behind the biggest asset class on earth.

Anatomy of a bond

A bond is a loan from the investor to the issuer. Four terms define it: face value (usually 100 or 1,000, repaid at maturity), coupon (the annual interest rate, paid semi-annually or annually), maturity (when principal is repaid), and yield (the market's required return, which moves every day). The coupon is fixed at issuance; the yield is set by the market — and the market price adjusts so that the two reconcile.

Price vs yield: the seesaw

A bond's price is the present value of all its future cash flows, discounted at the yield:

Price = Σ Coupont / (1 + y)t + Face / (1 + y)n

When yields rise, the discount rate rises, and the price falls — the two always move in opposite directions. A bond bought at par (price 100) with a 4% coupon falls below 100 the moment market yields climb above 4%.

Worked example. A 5-year bond, face value 100, 4% annual coupon, market yield 5%:

P = 4/1.05 + 4/1.05² + 4/1.05³ + 4/1.05⁴ + 104/1.05⁵ ≈ 95.67

The bond trades at a discount (95.67 < 100) because its 4% coupon is below the 5% the market demands. If yield fell to 3%, the same math gives ≈ 104.58 — a premium.

Yield curves: the market's forecast

Plotting yields against maturities gives the yield curve. Its shape matters:

  • Normal (upward sloping) — longer maturities pay more; investors expect growth and are compensated for time risk.
  • Inverted — short rates above long rates; historically a reliable recession warning, because the market expects central banks to cut rates later.
  • Flat — transition state; the market is undecided.

Support teams watch curve feeds closely: a missing or stale curve breaks pricing for every bond on the desk, since yields are interpolated from it.

Duration and convexity in one paragraph each

Duration measures price sensitivity: a duration of 7 means a 1% rise in yield drops the price roughly 7%. Longer maturities and lower coupons both increase duration. Convexity is the curvature — the correction term because the price-yield relationship is curved, not straight; it matters most for large yield moves and long-dated bonds.

Credit spreads

Corporate bonds yield more than government bonds of the same maturity. The difference is the credit spread — compensation for default risk. A bond yielding 6% against a 4% government benchmark trades at a 200bp spread. When spreads widen, corporate bond prices fall even if government yields do not move — a frequent source of "why did my bond drop?" tickets.
