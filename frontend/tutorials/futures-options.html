Futures & Options

Obligation versus right — the single distinction that explains both instruments.

Futures: a binding obligation

A futures contract obliges both sides to transact at a set price on a set date. Buy one crude-oil future at $80 and you must take delivery (or cash-settle) at $80 — no backing out. Because the obligation is symmetric, no premium changes hands upfront; instead both sides post margin, and gains and losses are settled daily (mark-to-market).

Worked example. You buy 1 S&P 500 future at 5,000 (contract multiplier $50, so $250,000 notional). The index closes at 5,050 — you are credited $50 × 50 = $2,500 that evening. If it had closed at 4,950, you would pay $2,500. There is nowhere to hide from a futures loss.

Futures exist on indices, commodities, bonds and currencies. Expiries follow quarterly cycles (March, June, September, December — the "IMM dates"), when the entire market rolls positions forward and volumes explode.

Options: a right, not an obligation

An option gives the buyer the right — never the obligation — to buy or sell at a set strike price before expiry. That asymmetry is why the buyer pays a premium upfront, and why the buyer's loss is capped at the premium while the upside can be large.

  • Call — the right to buy. Profits when the underlying rises above the strike.
  • Put — the right to sell. Profits when the underlying falls below the strike.

The premium has two parts: intrinsic value (how much the option is already in-the-money) and time value (what the remaining time might deliver — this decays every day, "theta decay").

Worked example — long call. You buy a call on XYZ, strike 100, premium 5. At expiry XYZ is at 110: you exercise, buy at 100, the share is worth 110 — payoff 10, minus the 5 premium = +5 profit. If XYZ closes at 95, you walk away: loss capped at the 5 premium.

Worked example — long put. You buy a put, strike 100, premium 4. XYZ crashes to 80: you sell at 100 what is worth 80 — payoff 20, minus 4 = +16 profit. Puts are the classic portfolio insurance.

Payoff intuition in one picture

A long future's P&L is a straight diagonal line through the entry price — symmetric. A long call's P&L is flat (at −premium) below the strike, then rises diagonally above it — the "hockey stick". A long put is the mirror image. Every exotic option payoff is just these sticks combined and repackaged.

What production support watches

  • Expiry processing — exercise, assignment and expiry jobs run on tight schedules; a stuck job leaves wrong positions overnight.
  • Margin calls — volatile days trigger intraday margin calls; downstream feeds must carry them to clients without delay.
  • Corporate actions — splits and dividends adjust strikes and multipliers; a missed adjustment corrupts every downstream valuation.
  • Volatility surfaces — option pricing needs the vol surface feed; a stale surface means every quote on the desk is wrong.
