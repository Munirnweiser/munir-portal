Futures & Options

Obligation versus right — the single distinction that explains both instruments.

Key takeaways

  • Futures are binding obligations with daily mark-to-market: a 50-point S&P move on one contract (×$50) is ±$2,500 credited that evening.
  • Options are rights, not obligations: long call payoff = max(S−K, 0) − premium; the loss is capped at the premium.
  • Greeks in practice: delta 0.55 on 10 contracts ≈ 550-share equivalent; theta bleeds −$30/day; vega $120 per vol point.
  • Margin is real money: 2 S&P futures need $27,500 initial margin; an $8,000 day-one loss leaves $19,500 equity.
  • Support hot spots: expiration and assignment (auto-exercise, pin risk), margin calls, and stale volatility surfaces.

Futures: a binding obligation

A futures contract obliges both sides to transact at a set price on a set date. Buy one crude-oil future at $80 and you must take delivery (or cash-settle) at $80 — no backing out. Because the obligation is symmetric, no premium changes hands upfront; instead both sides post margin, and gains and losses are settled daily (mark-to-market).

Worked example. You buy 1 S&P 500 future at 5,000 (contract multiplier $50, so $250,000 notional). The index closes at 5,050 — you are credited $50 × 50 = $2,500 that evening. If it had closed at 4,950, you would pay $2,500. There is nowhere to hide from a futures loss.

Futures exist on indices, commodities, bonds and currencies. Expiries follow quarterly cycles (March, June, September, December — the "IMM dates"), when the entire market rolls positions forward and volumes explode.

Options: a right, not an obligation

An option gives the buyer the right — never the obligation — to buy or sell at a set strike price before expiry. That asymmetry is why the buyer pays a premium upfront, and why the buyer's loss is capped at the premium while the upside can be large.

  • Call — the right to buy. Profits when the underlying rises above the strike.
  • Put — the right to sell. Profits when the underlying falls below the strike.

The premium has two parts: intrinsic value (how much the option is already in-the-money) and time value (what the remaining time might deliver — this decays every day, "theta decay").

Worked example — long call. You buy a call on XYZ, strike 100, premium 5. At expiry XYZ is at 110: you exercise, buy at 100, the share is worth 110 — payoff 10, minus the 5 premium = +5 profit. If XYZ closes at 95, you walk away: loss capped at the 5 premium.

Worked example — long put. You buy a put, strike 100, premium 4. XYZ crashes to 80: you sell at 100 what is worth 80 — payoff 20, minus 4 = +16 profit. Puts are the classic portfolio insurance.

id="sec-3"

Payoff intuition in one picture

A long future's P&L is a straight diagonal line through the entry price — symmetric. A long call's P&L is flat (at −premium) below the strike, then rises diagonally above it — the "hockey stick". A long put is the mirror image. Every exotic option payoff is just these sticks combined and repackaged.

What production support watches

  • Expiry processing — exercise, assignment and expiry jobs run on tight schedules; a stuck job leaves wrong positions overnight.
  • Margin calls — volatile days trigger intraday margin calls; downstream feeds must carry them to clients without delay.
  • Corporate actions — splits and dividends adjust strikes and multipliers; a missed adjustment corrupts every downstream valuation.
  • Volatility surfaces — option pricing needs the vol surface feed; a stale surface means every quote on the desk is wrong.

The Greeks, with numbers

Greeks translate option-speak into risk-speak. Take an illustrative 1-month call, strike 100, stock at 100, implied vol 25%:

GreekValue (per share)What it means
Delta0.55Behaves like 55 shares; gains $0.55 per $1 stock rise
Gamma0.045Delta rises 0.045 per $1 move — hedge must be rebalanced
Theta−0.03/dayLoses 3 cents of value every day, all else equal
Vega0.12Gains 12 cents per 1-point rise in implied vol

Worked example. A desk is long 10 contracts (1,000 shares) of this call: delta equivalent 550 shares, daily theta bleed −$30, vega $120 per vol point. If implied vol jumps 2 points overnight, the book gains ~$240 on vega — but theta keeps charging $30 every single day. Support angle: every Greek on the risk report is only as good as the vol surface and spot feeds behind it; a stale feed silently corrupts all four numbers at once.

Futures margin call: a worked example

Futures margin is a good-faith deposit, not a down payment. The exchange sets initial margin (to open) and a lower maintenance margin; fall below maintenance and you get a variation margin call — pay up immediately or be liquidated.

Worked example. You buy 2 S&P futures at 5,000 (multiplier $50 → $500,000 notional). Initial margin $13,750/contract, maintenance $12,500/contract — you post $27,500.

Day 1: index closes 4,920 → loss = 80 pts × $50 × 2 = $8,000
Equity = $27,500 − $8,000 = $19,500 < maintenance ($25,000) → margin call: $8,000

You wire $8,000 that evening, restoring equity to the initial $27,500 — not just to maintenance. Miss the call and the broker liquidates your position into the falling market, which is how orderly losses become disorderly ones.

Expiration and assignment: where support earns its pay

Expiry is the most operationally intense day in derivatives. Key mechanics support teams must know cold:

  • Auto-exercise: equity options are automatically exercised if $0.01 or more in-the-money at expiry — no instruction needed. Clients who wanted to abandon an ITM option must file a contrary exercise instruction before the cutoff.
  • Assignment lottery: writers of options do not choose whether they are assigned; the clearinghouse assigns randomly among open short positions. A client short 10 calls may wake up assigned on 3 — partial assignment is normal.
  • Pin risk: when the stock closes exactly at the strike, nobody knows until the last minute whether options finish in- or out-of-the-money. Desks hedge frantically into the close, and support watches the exercise/assignment jobs like a hawk.
  • Futures expiry: physically-settled contracts require delivery logistics; cash-settled ones need the final settlement price feed — a wrong settlement price corrupts every expiring position.

Support watches: the expiry job schedule (exercise → assignment → position conversion), cutoff times for contrary instructions, and the reconciliation between the clearinghouse assignment file and internal positions before the next open.

Practice: easy, medium, hard

Try these before moving on — click a question to reveal the answer.

Easy

1. Buy 1 S&P 500 future at 5,000 (multiplier $50); it closes at 5,050. P&L?

Answer: +$2,500 credited that evening: (5,050 − 5,000) × $50.

2. Long call, strike 100, premium 5; the stock closes at 95 at expiry. Result?

Answer: Walk away — the loss is capped at the 5 premium.

Medium

3. Long call, strike 100, premium 5; stock at 110 at expiry. Profit?

Answer: Payoff 10 minus 5 premium = +5 profit.

4. 2 S&P futures bought at 5,000; day-one close 4,920; initial margin posted $27,500. New equity?

Answer: Loss = 80 × $50 × 2 = $8,000 → equity $19,500.

Hard

5. Desk is long 10 calls: delta 0.55 each, theta −$30/day total, vega $120/vol-point total. Implied vol jumps 2 points overnight. Approximate P&L?

Answer: ≈ +$240 on vega, minus the $30 daily theta bleed.

6. Expiry Friday, stock pinned exactly at 100, clients are long 100-strike calls. What should support expect?

Answer: Pin risk: the auto-exercise lottery, assignment notices over the weekend, and Monday reconciliation breaks if the back office isn't ready.
