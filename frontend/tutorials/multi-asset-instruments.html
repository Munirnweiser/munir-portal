Multi-Asset Instruments

One desk trades them all: equities, bonds, FX, commodities and derivatives — what each instrument is, how it settles, and where support tickets come from.

Key takeaways

  • Equities settle T+1: 10,000 shares at $50 on Monday means $500,000 against 10,000 shares on Tuesday — wrong SSI fails the delivery.
  • ETF arbitrage keeps price near NAV: a $5M basket at $100 NAV trading at $100.40 lets the AP create and pocket ≈ $20k.
  • ADR dividends leak value: €1.00 declared becomes ≈ $1,050 per 1,000 ADRs after FX, custody fees and withholding tax.
  • Contango bleeds rollers: rolling 1,000 crude contracts can cost $2M a quarter — negative roll yield is real P&L.
  • Rate swaps move real money: pay 4.00% fixed / receive SOFR on $100M nets ≈ $500k a year when SOFR averages 4.50%.

Equities: shares, ETFs and ADRs

An ordinary share is part-ownership of a company. An ETF is a fund that trades like a share and tracks an index or basket. An ADR (American Depositary Receipt) lets a US investor hold a foreign share — the foreign shares sit with a custodian bank, and the ADR trades in USD in New York.

Cash equities settle T+1 in the US and Canada (T+2 in much of Europe and Asia). Corporate actions — dividends, splits, mergers — are the classic support headache: a stock split changes every open order's price and quantity, and a missed corporate-action feed means positions show the wrong economics.

Worked example. A client buys 10,000 shares of a stock at $50.00 on Monday. Settlement is Tuesday (T+1): $500,000 of cash moves against 10,000 shares through the clearinghouse. If the client's custodian details (SSI) are wrong, the delivery fails and the trade sits in a fail queue accruing charges.

Fixed income: bonds, T-bills and repos

Bonds pay coupons and return principal at maturity; T-bills are short-term discount paper with no coupon. A repo (repurchase agreement) is a collateralized loan: sell a bond today, agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price — the difference is the repo rate, the market's overnight funding rate.

Bonds settle T+1 (US Treasuries) or T+2 (many corporates). Accrued interest must be computed exactly — day-count conventions (30/360, ACT/ACT) differ by market, and a wrong convention breaks the settlement amount by the coupon accrual.

FX: spot, forwards, swaps and NDFs

FX spot settles T+2 (T+1 for USD/CAD); forwards and swaps settle on their agreed value dates; NDFs cash-settle in USD against an official fixing. FX is the highest-volume asset class in the world, and settlement runs through CLS Bank on hard intraday deadlines.

Support pain points: missed CLS pay-in deadlines, late fixings for NDF valuation, and value dates falling on currency holidays — each creates fails that cascade into funding costs.

id="sec-4"

Commodities: futures and physical

Commodity futures (oil, gas, metals, agriculture) are exchange-traded contracts for delivery at a future date, margined daily — gains and losses are settled in cash every day. Physical commodities add logistics: storage, delivery location, and quality grades.

Watch for expiry handling: a future approaching its last trading day must be rolled or closed, and first-notice-day rules differ per exchange. A position left into delivery by mistake becomes an operations incident, not a trading decision.

id="sec-5"

Derivatives: options, swaps, CDS and exotics

Options give the right (not the obligation) to buy or sell; swaps exchange cash flows (fixed-for-floating rates, or currencies); a CDS (credit default swap) is insurance against a borrower defaulting; exotics add path-dependent or barrier features. Listed derivatives are margined daily; OTC derivatives are collateralized bilaterally or cleared.

Worked example. A 1-year interest-rate swap: the bank pays fixed 4.00% and receives floating SOFR on $100M notional. If SOFR averages 4.50%, the bank nets roughly $500,000 over the year. The trade's value moves with the rate curve, so a stale curve in the pricing engine misprices every swap on the book at once.

ETF mechanics: creation and redemption

An ETF's market price stays near its net asset value because of authorized participants (APs) — large dealers who can create or redeem ETF shares directly with the fund. When the ETF trades at a premium, the AP buys the underlying basket of stocks, delivers it to the fund, and receives new ETF shares to sell at the premium. When it trades at a discount, the AP does the reverse: buys cheap ETF shares, redeems them for the underlying stocks, and sells the stocks. This arbitrage is what keeps the price glued to NAV.

Creations and redemptions happen in large blocks called creation units (often 50,000 shares) and are usually in-kind — securities for securities, not cash — which is why ETFs are tax-efficient. Support angle: if creations are halted (a corporate action on a basket constituent, a trading halt), the premium or discount can gap; a desk showing a stale NAV during a halt misprices every quote.

Worked example. An ETF holds a basket worth $5,000,000 per creation unit (50,000 shares → NAV $100/share). The ETF trades at $100.40 — a 0.4% premium. The AP buys the basket for $5,000,000, creates 50,000 shares, sells them for $5,020,000, and pockets roughly $20,000 minus costs. Repeated until the premium closes.

ADR vs GDR: same idea, different plumbing

Both let investors hold foreign shares locally, but the plumbing differs. An ADR trades in the US in USD — a custodian bank holds the foreign shares and issues receipts; the ratio (e.g., 1 ADR = 2 ordinary shares) keeps the price in a sensible range. A GDR typically lists in London or Luxembourg, often in USD or EUR, and frequently comes in two tranches: Reg S (non-US investors) and 144A (US institutions only).

The costs investors forget: the depositary charges a custody fee — typically $0.01 to $0.03 per ADR per year, deducted from dividends — and every dividend is converted from the local currency at the depositary's FX rate, not the market rate.

Worked example. A French company declares a €1.00 dividend. You hold 1,000 ADRs (ratio 1:1). The depositary converts at its own rate (€1 = $1.07 instead of the market $1.085), takes a $0.02/ADR custody fee, and you receive roughly $1,050 instead of $1,085. Support tickets about "wrong dividend amounts" on ADRs are almost always the fee and the FX conversion, not a data error.

Futures curves: contango and backwardation

Futures for successive expiries form a curve. In contango the curve slopes upward — later contracts cost more, reflecting storage and financing costs for a storable commodity. In backwardation it slopes downward — near-term contracts command a premium, usually because of immediate scarcity (a supply disruption, low inventories).

The curve decides the economics of rolling: a long position rolled in contango sells cheap front-month and buys expensive deferred — a steady bleed called negative roll yield. In backwardation the roll earns. Support angle: expiry calendars and roll dates are reference data; a missed roll turns a futures position into a delivery obligation.

Worked example. Crude oil: front-month $78.00, 3-month $80.00, 6-month $81.50 — contango of $3.50 over six months, roughly the cost of storing and financing a barrel. Rolling 1,000 contracts from front-month to 3-month costs about ($80.00 − $78.00) × 1,000 contracts × 1,000 barrels = $2,000,000 of roll bleed each quarter the curve stays shaped this way.

id="sec-9"

What production support watches

  • Settlement calendars — every asset class has its own holiday calendars; a bad calendar breaks value dates across the whole book.
  • Reference data — wrong day-count, wrong fixing source, missing corporate action: most "trade breaks" are data breaks wearing a trade's clothes.
  • Margin calls — futures and cleared swaps margin daily; a failed margin feed means the desk flies blind on funding needs.
  • Cross-asset knock-ons — an FX fail delays a bond settlement funded in another currency; always check upstream when a downstream trade breaks.

Practice: easy, medium, hard

Try these before moving on — click a question to reveal the answer.

Easy

1. A client buys 10,000 shares at $50.00 on Monday. When does it settle and for how much?

Answer: Tuesday (T+1): $500,000 of cash against 10,000 shares through the clearinghouse.

2. An ETF's NAV is $100 but it trades at $100.40. What does the authorized participant do?

Answer: Buys the $5M basket, creates 50,000 shares, sells them at $100.40 — capturing the ≈ $20,000 premium.

Medium

3. 1-year interest-rate swap: bank pays fixed 4.00%, receives SOFR on $100M notional; SOFR averages 4.50%. Net result?

Answer: Roughly +$500,000 for the year: (4.50% − 4.00%) × $100M.

4. Explain the $2M/quarter contango bleed on 1,000 crude contracts.

Answer: Each roll sells the cheap front-month future and buys the pricier deferred one — the negative roll yield compounds into real losses.

Hard

5. An ADR declares a €1.00 dividend; the holder receives ≈ $1,050 per 1,000 ADRs. Account for the leakage.

Answer: FX conversion at the depositary's rate, minus custody fees and dividend withholding tax.

6. A CDS credit event is declared on a name in the book. Walk the support impact.

Answer: Positions terminate via auction/cash settlement; P&L, risk and downstream feeds must book the event the same day or everything downstream is wrong.
