Multi-Asset Instruments

One desk trades them all: equities, bonds, FX, commodities and derivatives — what each instrument is, how it settles, and where support tickets come from.

Equities: shares, ETFs and ADRs

An ordinary share is part-ownership of a company. An ETF is a fund that trades like a share and tracks an index or basket. An ADR (American Depositary Receipt) lets a US investor hold a foreign share — the foreign shares sit with a custodian bank, and the ADR trades in USD in New York.

Cash equities settle T+1 in the US and Canada (T+2 in much of Europe and Asia). Corporate actions — dividends, splits, mergers — are the classic support headache: a stock split changes every open order's price and quantity, and a missed corporate-action feed means positions show the wrong economics.

Worked example. A client buys 10,000 shares of a stock at $50.00 on Monday. Settlement is Tuesday (T+1): $500,000 of cash moves against 10,000 shares through the clearinghouse. If the client's custodian details (SSI) are wrong, the delivery fails and the trade sits in a fail queue accruing charges.

Fixed income: bonds, T-bills and repos

Bonds pay coupons and return principal at maturity; T-bills are short-term discount paper with no coupon. A repo (repurchase agreement) is a collateralized loan: sell a bond today, agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price — the difference is the repo rate, the market's overnight funding rate.

Bonds settle T+1 (US Treasuries) or T+2 (many corporates). Accrued interest must be computed exactly — day-count conventions (30/360, ACT/ACT) differ by market, and a wrong convention breaks the settlement amount by the coupon accrual.

FX: spot, forwards, swaps and NDFs

FX spot settles T+2 (T+1 for USD/CAD); forwards and swaps settle on their agreed value dates; NDFs cash-settle in USD against an official fixing. FX is the highest-volume asset class in the world, and settlement runs through CLS Bank on hard intraday deadlines.

Support pain points: missed CLS pay-in deadlines, late fixings for NDF valuation, and value dates falling on currency holidays — each creates fails that cascade into funding costs.

Commodities: futures and physical

Commodity futures (oil, gas, metals, agriculture) are exchange-traded contracts for delivery at a future date, margined daily — gains and losses are settled in cash every day. Physical commodities add logistics: storage, delivery location, and quality grades.

Watch for expiry handling: a future approaching its last trading day must be rolled or closed, and first-notice-day rules differ per exchange. A position left into delivery by mistake becomes an operations incident, not a trading decision.

Derivatives: options, swaps, CDS and exotics

Options give the right (not the obligation) to buy or sell; swaps exchange cash flows (fixed-for-floating rates, or currencies); a CDS (credit default swap) is insurance against a borrower defaulting; exotics add path-dependent or barrier features. Listed derivatives are margined daily; OTC derivatives are collateralized bilaterally or cleared.

Worked example. A 1-year interest-rate swap: the bank pays fixed 4.00% and receives floating SOFR on $100M notional. If SOFR averages 4.50%, the bank nets roughly $500,000 over the year. The trade's value moves with the rate curve, so a stale curve in the pricing engine misprices every swap on the book at once.

What production support watches

  • Settlement calendars — every asset class has its own holiday calendars; a bad calendar breaks value dates across the whole book.
  • Reference data — wrong day-count, wrong fixing source, missing corporate action: most "trade breaks" are data breaks wearing a trade's clothes.
  • Margin calls — futures and cleared swaps margin daily; a failed margin feed means the desk flies blind on funding needs.
  • Cross-asset knock-ons — an FX fail delays a bond settlement funded in another currency; always check upstream when a downstream trade breaks.
