Risk Measures

Delta, Gamma, Vega and VaR — the numbers risk engines compute all day, with worked examples.

Learning path Markets & Trading Track 8 of 9

Key takeaways

  • Delta hedge: +2,500 shares of delta → sell 2,500 shares → delta neutral.
  • Gamma moves delta: 0.50 + 0.08 × $1 = 0.58 — on a big book the hedge decays within minutes.
  • Parametric VaR: $10M × 1.2% vol × 1.65 ≈ $198k one-day 95%; scale with √10 → ≈ $626k ten-day.
  • Historical VaR: worst 1-in-10 of ten observations = $45k — but tiny samples, regime blindness and ghost effects make it fragile.
  • Stress scenarios catch what VaR misses: $80k DV01 × 150bp shock ≈ $12M.

Delta: directional exposure

Delta is how much a position's value moves when the underlying moves by 1 unit. A long call with delta 0.60 gains about $0.60 per $1 rise in the stock. Portfolio delta is additive, which makes it the first number every risk report shows.

Worked example. A desk holds options with total delta +2,500 shares equivalent. The trader sells 2,500 shares of the underlying — portfolio delta becomes ~0 ("delta neutral"). If the stock jumps $2, the unhedged book would have made $5,000; hedged, the option gains and the short stock loses roughly offset.

Gamma: how delta itself moves

Gamma is the rate of change of delta — the curvature. High gamma means delta changes fast, so a "delta neutral" book does not stay neutral for long. Gamma is highest for at-the-money options near expiry, which is why expiry days see frantic hedging: market makers chase deltas as the underlying whipsaws.

Worked example. A call has delta 0.50 and gamma 0.08. The stock rises $1: new delta ≈ 0.50 + 0.08 × 1 = 0.58. The hedge that was correct a minute ago is now 8 deltas light per contract — on 10,000 contracts that is 800 shares to buy, now.

Vega: sensitivity to volatility

Vega measures value change per 1-point move in implied volatility. Long options are long vega — they gain when volatility rises. A book with vega of $50,000 makes roughly $50,000 if implied vol rises one point across the board. Vega is why option desks care as much about the vol surface feed as the price feed: a stale surface misprices every option on the book.

Value at Risk: "how bad could tomorrow be?"

VaR answers: the maximum expected loss over a horizon at a given confidence. "1-day 95% VaR of $200k" means losses should exceed $200k only about 1 day in 20. Two ways to compute it:

  • Parametric VaR — assumes returns are roughly normal: VaR = 1.65 × σ × portfolio value (1.65 is the 95% normal quantile).
  • Historical VaR — replay the last N days of actual market moves through today's portfolio and take the 5th-percentile worst outcome. No normality assumption, but hostage to whatever history contains.

Worked example (parametric). A $10M equity portfolio with 1.2% daily volatility: 1-day 95% VaR = 1.65 × 0.012 × $10,000,000 ≈ $198,000. Scale to 10 days with the square-root-of-time rule: $198,000 × √10 ≈ $626,000.

VaR's blind spot: it says nothing about how bad the worst 5% gets — that is what Expected Shortfall (average loss beyond VaR) addresses, and why stress scenarios exist alongside it.

What production support watches

  • Risk batch windows — overnight VaR and sensitivity batches must finish before traders arrive; a delayed batch means trading blind.
  • Feed staleness — vol surfaces, curves and spot feeds timestamped; anything older than tolerance invalidates the Greeks.
  • Limit breaches — VaR or delta limit alerts must reach the desk in real time; a silent alerting pipeline is a finding in every audit.

Historical VaR, computed by hand

Historical VaR makes no normality assumption — it replays real history. Take 10 days of a desk's P&L in $k, sorted worst to best:

−45, −30, −18, −12, −5, +3, +8, +15, +22, +40

Worked example. For 95% VaR with 10 observations, take the worst 1-in-10 outcome: $45k. Interpretation: on the worst 10% of days in this sample, the desk lost at least $45k.

Now the catches a support analyst should know: (1) sample size — 10 days is far too few; production uses 250–500 days, where 95% VaR is the 13th–25th worst day; (2) regime blindness — if the sample contains no crisis, VaR has never seen one; (3) ghost effects — when a huge loss finally drops out of the rolling window, VaR can fall overnight with no change in risk. Every one of these has generated a "VaR moved but nothing changed" ticket.

Designing stress scenarios that actually stress

VaR describes the ordinary bad days; stress tests describe the extraordinary ones. A serious program runs three families:

  • Historical replays — 2008 (credit freeze, +40bp daily spread moves), March 2020 (vol explosion, −12% equity days), 1994 (bond massacre). Real, defensible, but backward-looking.
  • Hypothetical shocks — rates +200bp parallel, equities −30%, vol doubled, FX ±15%, oil +50%. Forward-looking, but someone must defend the calibration.
  • Reverse stress tests — instead of "what does this shock do?", ask "what shock breaks us?" Find the loss that wipes out the buffer, then work backwards to the market move. Regulators love these because they expose hidden concentrations.

Worked example. A rates desk with DV01 of $80k (loses $80k per 1bp rise) runs a "+150bp in a week" scenario: 150 × $80k = $12M loss — before convexity and liquidity effects. If the desk's loss limit is $10M, the scenario breaches it, and the risk committee gets a pre-emptive conversation instead of a post-mortem.

Anatomy of a limit-monitoring dashboard

What does the risk team actually stare at all day? A limit dashboard is typically organized as:

  • Headline tiles — firm VaR vs limit ($8.2M / $10M), utilization %, trend arrow vs yesterday.
  • Desk breakdown — VaR, delta, vega per desk with traffic-light coloring; drill-down to trader level.
  • Concentration — top-5 positions by risk contribution; single-name and sector caps.
  • Exceptions log — every breach with timestamp, owner, and status (acknowledged / action plan / escalated).
  • Data health — feed timestamps and batch completion flags; a risk number computed on stale data is worse than no number.

Support owns the plumbing: the overnight risk batch window (must complete before traders arrive), real-time limit-alert delivery (a silent alerting pipeline is an audit finding), and the reference-data joins that attribute risk to the right desk. When a trader says "my limit looks wrong," the answer is in the batch logs and feed timestamps — always check data health before doubting the math.

Easy

1. A desk's option book has delta +2,500 shares equivalent. How do you hedge it?

Answer: Sell 2,500 shares of the underlying — portfolio delta becomes ≈ 0 (delta neutral).

2. A call has delta 0.50 and gamma 0.08. The stock rises $1. New delta?

Answer: ≈ 0.50 + 0.08 × 1 = 0.58.

Medium

3. Compute one-day 95% parametric VaR for a $10M equity portfolio with 1.2% daily volatility.

Answer: 1.65 × 0.012 × $10,000,000 ≈ $198,000.

4. Scale that VaR to a 10-day horizon.

Answer: $198,000 × √10 ≈ $626,000.

Hard

5. Historical VaR from 10 observations gives $45k (worst 1-in-10). Why is this fragile?

Answer: Tiny sample, regime blindness (ten calm days say nothing about crises), and ghost effects — one old outlier dominates the estimate.

6. DV01 is $80k and rates shock +150bp. Expected loss — and what does this catch that VaR misses?

Answer: ≈ $12M. Stress scenarios catch tail moves outside the normal-volatility world VaR assumes.
