Valuation Methods

DCF, multiples, bond and option valuation — how the market decides what something is worth, with the math kept honest.

DCF: value is future cash, discounted

Discounted cash flow values an asset as the present value of the cash it will generate. The simplest form — a perpetuity growing at rate g, discounted at r — is the Gordon growth formula:

Value = CF₁ / (r − g)

Worked example. A business generates $100M free cash flow next year, growing 3% annually, discount rate 8%:

Value = 100 / (0.08 − 0.03) = $2,000M

Note the sensitivity: raise the discount rate to 9% and value falls to $1,667M — a one-point move costs 17%. Valuation is an opinion about r and g wearing a formula's clothes.

Multiples: value by comparison

Instead of forecasting cash flows, multiples price an asset relative to peers: P/E (price per dollar of earnings), EV/EBITDA (enterprise value per dollar of operating profit). If comparable banks trade at 12× earnings and yours earns $5/share, the multiple implies $60/share.

Multiples are fast and market-grounded, but they inherit the market's mood — in a bubble, every multiple says "fair value" right up until it doesn't. Good analysts use multiples to check a DCF, never to replace one.

Bond valuation: discount the coupons

A bond is worth the present value of its coupons plus principal, discounted at the market yield — the same seesaw from the fixed-income tutorial. The practical wrinkle is which yield: each cash flow is really discounted at its own point on the curve (bootstrapped spot rates), though a single yield-to-maturity is the market's shorthand quote.

Option valuation: Black-Scholes intuition, no heavy math

The Black-Scholes model prices an option from five inputs — and the intuition for each is straightforward:

  • Underlying price — higher spot, more valuable call, less valuable put.
  • Strike — further out-of-the-money, cheaper.
  • Time to expiry — more time, more chance, higher premium (theta decay eats it daily).
  • Volatility — the big one; higher expected wiggle means higher option value in both directions.
  • Interest rates — higher rates lift call values slightly (deferred payment is cheaper).

The model assumes lognormal prices and constant volatility — both false, both useful. The market's real quote is implied volatility: the vol number that makes the model reproduce the traded price. When traders say "vol is bid," they mean options are expensive relative to history.

Worked example (intuition). A 1-month at-the-money call on a $100 stock with 20% implied vol costs roughly $2.30. Double the vol to 40% and the same call costs roughly $4.60 — option value scales almost linearly with volatility for short-dated at-the-money options. That is vega in action.

Black-Scholes, worked end to end

Time to run the actual formula. Price a 6-month European call: stock S = 100, strike K = 100, risk-free rate r = 5%, volatility σ = 25%, time T = 0.5 years.

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T] / (σ√T)
d2 = d1 − σ√T
Call = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)

Worked example.

d1 = [0 + (0.05 + 0.03125) × 0.5] / (0.25 × 0.7071) = 0.040625 / 0.17678 ≈ 0.23
d2 = 0.23 − 0.17678 ≈ 0.05
N(d1) ≈ 0.5909,   N(d2) ≈ 0.5211
Call = 100 × 0.5909 − 100 × e−0.025 × 0.5211 ≈ 59.09 − 50.82 = $8.27

N(d1) ≈ 0.59 is also the option's delta — the model hands you the hedge ratio for free. And note what moves the price most: bump σ to 35% and the call reprices near $10.90 — a reminder that the vol input dominates the model output, which is why desks obsess over the vol surface feed.

Binomial trees: Black-Scholes without calculus

A binomial tree prices options by brute force: the stock can go up or down each step; work backwards from expiry, discounting expected payoffs at the risk-free rate using risk-neutral probabilities. As the steps multiply, the tree converges to the Black-Scholes price — same answer, no calculus.

Worked example (one step). Stock at 100; in one period it goes to 110 (up) or 90 (down). Risk-free rate 5% per period. Price a 100-strike call expiring then.

Risk-neutral p = (1.05 − 0.90) / (1.10 − 0.90) = 0.75
Payoffs: up → max(110−100, 0) = 10; down → 0
Call = (0.75 × 10 + 0.25 × 0) / 1.05 ≈ $7.14

Trees shine where Black-Scholes cannot go: American options (early exercise — check at each node whether exercising beats holding) and path-dependent payoffs. Support relevance: pricing-library upgrades are often tree-calibration changes, and a miscalibrated tree reprices the whole book silently.

Dirty vs clean price: a worked settlement example

Bonds are quoted clean but settle dirty — clean price plus accrued interest. From the fixed-income tutorial's 30/360 example: 5% annual coupon, last coupon 15 January, settlement 15 May (120 days), clean price 98.00.

Worked example.

Accrued = 5.00 × 120/360 = 1.67 per 100
Dirty price = 98.00 + 1.67 = 99.67

On a $5M face trade the buyer wires $4,983,500 — not $4,900,000. Three support classics live here: (1) booking the clean price as the settlement amount, (2) day-count convention mismatches between counterparties (30/360 vs actual/actual can differ by days), and (3) accrued going negative logic errors when settlement predates the last coupon in the system.

Bootstrapping a discount curve, worked

A discount curve is built one maturity at a time from market prices — each new point "bootstrapped" off the previous ones. Given a 1-year zero rate of 4.00% and a 2-year par rate of 4.50% (annual coupons, par 100):

Worked example. DF(1) = 1 / 1.04 = 0.96154. The 2-year par bond satisfies: 100 = 4.50 × DF(1) + 104.50 × DF(2). So 104.50 × DF(2) = 100 − 4.50 × 0.96154 = 95.6731, giving DF(2) = 0.91552. The implied 2-year zero: (1/0.91552)1/2 − 1 ≈ 4.51%.

Support relevance: if the pricer builds its curve from a fixing file that's missing today, every discount factor shifts and every bond reprices. When marks look "yesterday-ish," the curve's as-of date is the first thing to check — before the model, before the market data, before anything.

Incident: the curve that didn't roll

Symptom. Tuesday 08:00 — bond desk marks look stale; P&L is flat despite a 10bp overnight rate move. Traders insist "the market moved, our books didn't."

  • Diagnose: the curve-build job failed at 06:30 — the log reads missing 3M fixing; vendor file arrived 06:45, after the 06:30 cutoff. The pricer silently fell back to yesterday's curve. Check: the curve "as-of" timestamp in the risk database vs today.
  • Root cause: a hard cutoff with a silent fallback — the job failed, the pricer shrugged, and every downstream number lied for the whole morning.
  • Fix: re-run the curve build with the late file, reprice, republish risk. Then make the fallback loud: alert on "curve as-of ≠ today" and page if the build fails — a system that fails loudly at 06:31 beats one that lies quietly all day.

Lesson. Silent fallbacks are the most dangerous failure mode in pricing. Any job whose output feeds valuation must either succeed on today's inputs or wake someone up — there is no acceptable third option.

Interview Q&A: model vs market — who's wrong?

Q: Your model price and the market price disagree by 2%. Is the model wrong?

Strong answer: "Not necessarily — first I check which one is stale. (1) Is the market price a real trade or an indicative quote? Indications on illiquid bonds can be 2% off reality. (2) Are the model inputs current — curve as-of date, volatility surface, credit spread? (3) Clean or dirty — is one side quoting accrued and the other isn't? That explains small gaps instantly. (4) Check the calendar — ex-dividend and coupon effects. Only after all four do I question the model itself. In support, nine out of ten 'the model is wrong' tickets turn out to be stale inputs or clean/dirty confusion — the model is guilty last, not first."
