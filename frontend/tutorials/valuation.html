Valuation Methods

DCF, multiples, bond and option valuation — how the market decides what something is worth, with the math kept honest.

DCF: value is future cash, discounted

Discounted cash flow values an asset as the present value of the cash it will generate. The simplest form — a perpetuity growing at rate g, discounted at r — is the Gordon growth formula:

Value = CF₁ / (r − g)

Worked example. A business generates $100M free cash flow next year, growing 3% annually, discount rate 8%:

Value = 100 / (0.08 − 0.03) = $2,000M

Note the sensitivity: raise the discount rate to 9% and value falls to $1,667M — a one-point move costs 17%. Valuation is an opinion about r and g wearing a formula's clothes.

Multiples: value by comparison

Instead of forecasting cash flows, multiples price an asset relative to peers: P/E (price per dollar of earnings), EV/EBITDA (enterprise value per dollar of operating profit). If comparable banks trade at 12× earnings and yours earns $5/share, the multiple implies $60/share.

Multiples are fast and market-grounded, but they inherit the market's mood — in a bubble, every multiple says "fair value" right up until it doesn't. Good analysts use multiples to check a DCF, never to replace one.

Bond valuation: discount the coupons

A bond is worth the present value of its coupons plus principal, discounted at the market yield — the same seesaw from the fixed-income tutorial. The practical wrinkle is which yield: each cash flow is really discounted at its own point on the curve (bootstrapped spot rates), though a single yield-to-maturity is the market's shorthand quote.

Option valuation: Black-Scholes intuition, no heavy math

The Black-Scholes model prices an option from five inputs — and the intuition for each is straightforward:

  • Underlying price — higher spot, more valuable call, less valuable put.
  • Strike — further out-of-the-money, cheaper.
  • Time to expiry — more time, more chance, higher premium (theta decay eats it daily).
  • Volatility — the big one; higher expected wiggle means higher option value in both directions.
  • Interest rates — higher rates lift call values slightly (deferred payment is cheaper).

The model assumes lognormal prices and constant volatility — both false, both useful. The market's real quote is implied volatility: the vol number that makes the model reproduce the traded price. When traders say "vol is bid," they mean options are expensive relative to history.

Worked example (intuition). A 1-month at-the-money call on a $100 stock with 20% implied vol costs roughly $2.30. Double the vol to 40% and the same call costs roughly $4.60 — option value scales almost linearly with volatility for short-dated at-the-money options. That is vega in action.
