Risk Measures: VaR & Stress

VaR, Stressed VaR, stress tests and Expected Shortfall — the numbers risk engines produce every night, and what support does when they look wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Stressed VaR recalibrates on a crisis window: 3.8% vol (2008) → $2.8M ten-day 99% — capital follows the stressed number, not the calm one.

Parametric VaR: the formula approach

Value at Risk answers: "what is the most we can lose over a horizon, with a given confidence?" Parametric VaR assumes returns are normally distributed:

VaR = z × σ × √t × portfolio value

where z is 1.65 for 95% confidence (2.33 for 99%), σ is daily volatility, and t is the horizon in days.

Worked example. A $10M equity book with 1.2% daily volatility. 1-day 95% VaR = 1.65 × 0.012 × $10M = $198,000. For a 10-day horizon (the regulatory standard), scale by √10: $198,000 × 3.162 ≈ $626,000.

Interpretation: on 95 out of 100 days, the loss should not exceed $198,000. On the other 5 days, it can — and VaR says nothing about how bad those days get.

Historical VaR: let the data speak

Historical VaR makes no normality assumption. Take the last 250 daily P&L observations, sort worst to best, and read off the loss at the 5% tail — the 13th worst day (5% of 250 = 12.5). That loss is the 1-day 95% historical VaR.

It captures fat tails and real market behavior, but it is only as good as its window: a calm year produces a VaR that understates risk the moment volatility returns. That weakness is exactly why regulators added Stressed VaR.

Stressed VaR (SVaR): VaR in a crisis

SVaR runs the same VaR calculation but calibrated on a continuous 12-month period of significant market stress (2008, March 2020). Because stressed volatility is far higher, SVaR is typically 2–4× normal VaR — it answers "what would our risk look like if 2008 happened to today's book?"

Worked example. The same $10M book: normal 10-day 99% VaR = $450,000. Recalibrated on a 2008 stress window, daily vol jumps to 3.8%: SVaR = 2.33 × 0.038 × √10 × $10M ≈ $2.8M. Capital requirements are driven by the stressed number, not the calm one.

Stress testing and scenario analysis

Stress tests skip probability entirely and ask "what if?": apply a defined shock to every position and revalue. Typical scenarios: +200bp parallel rate shift, −30% global equities, oil +50%, credit spreads ×3. Unlike VaR, a stress test shows the actual dollar loss under each named scenario — no confidence level, no distribution, just the number.

Worked example. A bond portfolio with $1.2M DV01 (dollar loss per 1bp rate rise) under a +200bp shock loses roughly 200 × $1.2M = $240M before convexity. Risk committees live on these tables; when the overnight stress run fails, the desk has no official numbers at the morning meeting.

Expected Shortfall and backtesting

Expected Shortfall (ES) fixes VaR's blind spot: it is the average loss on the days VaR is breached — the tail beyond the line. Backtesting checks the model against reality: count how many times the actual loss exceeded 1-day 99% VaR over 250 days. Regulators use traffic lights: 0–4 exceptions = green, 5–9 = yellow (capital add-on), 10+ = red (model rejected).

Backtesting traffic lights, in detail

Regulators backtest 1-day 99% VaR over 250 trading days. With 99% confidence, about 2.5 exceptions are expected even from a perfect model — exceptions follow a binomial distribution, and the chance of 5 or more exceptions from a correct model is roughly 10%. That is why the zones scale instead of failing outright.

  • Green (0–4 exceptions) — model accepted; capital multiplier 3.0.
  • Yellow (5–9 exceptions) — capital add-on grows with each exception: 5 → 3.4, 6 → 3.5, 7 → 3.65, 8 → 3.75, 9 → 3.85.
  • Red (10+ exceptions) — multiplier up to 4.0 and the model is presumed broken; supervisors can force a return to the standardized approach.

Worked example. A desk records 7 exceptions in 250 days. Yellow zone: multiplier 3.65 instead of 3.0, so market-risk capital rises about 22% overnight. Support angle: backtesting is a scheduled batch — if the exceptions report doesn't run, the desk discovers its zone from the regulator, not from risk.

Choosing the stressed period: the rationale

SVaR must be calibrated on a continuous 12-month window of significant stress that is relevant to the current portfolio — and the choice must be reviewed at least annually. The rationale is anti-procyclicality: capital set only on calm-period VaR collapses exactly when it is most needed, then spikes in a crisis. A fixed stress window keeps a crisis-sized charge on the books through the good years.

The subtlety is relevance: a rates-heavy book calibrated on the 2008 credit crisis may understate its risk, while an equities book calibrated on March 2020 is well covered. Supervisors expect documented justification, and the re-selection is a scheduled annual job — miss it and it becomes a regulatory finding, not a modeling debate.

Expected Shortfall, worked

Expected Shortfall at 99% is the average loss over the worst 1% of outcomes — the tail VaR ignores. It is always at least as large as VaR, and unlike VaR it is coherent: it aggregates properly across books, while VaR can claim two risky books are safer combined than apart.

Worked example. 250 daily P&L observations, worst three days: −$410,000, −$380,000, −$350,000. The 99% VaR (roughly the 3rd worst) is $350,000; the 99% ES is the average of the tail: ($410,000 + $380,000 + $350,000) / 3 = $380,000. A risk committee shown only the $350,000 VaR has no idea the average breach is $30,000 worse.

FRTB in one paragraph: internal models vs standardized

The Fundamental Review of the Trading Book replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% Expected Shortfall as the regulatory standard and gave banks two ways to compute capital. The internal models approach (IMA) lets a bank use its own ES models — but each trading desk must pass P&L attribution tests and backtesting, and failing desks fall back. The standardized approach (SA) is the regulator's prescribed sensitivities-based calculation: always available, no approval needed, usually more punitive capital. Desks can be split across the two, and support teams feel it the same way: SA and IMA numbers both come from overnight batches, and a feed break corrupts both.

What production support watches

  • Risk-feed breaks — VaR engines consume positions, market data and curves; a missing feed means stale or missing risk numbers, and traders notice before risk managers do.
  • Limit breaches — when VaR exceeds its limit, the alert must fire and reach the desk; a silent breach is a support incident with regulatory overtones.
  • Overnight batch failures — SVaR and stress runs are heavy jobs; a failed run at 3 AM needs triage before the 7 AM risk meeting, not after.
  • Model vs market divergence — repeated backtesting exceptions mean the model is wrong, not the market; escalate to quantitative risk, don't just restart the job.

A note on backtesting Expected Shortfall

VaR is backtested by counting exceptions - clean and simple. Expected Shortfall is harder: ES is not elicitable, meaning no scoring function is minimized only by the true ES. In practice, desks backtest the VaR that ES is conditioned on, then apply the Acerbi-Szekely test statistic, which checks whether realized tail losses are consistent with the reported ES.

What this means for support: risk reports carry both numbers, and the validation jobs that check them are different jobs with different failure modes. When the ES validation feed breaks but VaR is fine, the report still goes out - flagged. Know which downstream consumers read the flag and which ignore it.
