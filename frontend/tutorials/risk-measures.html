Risk Measures: VaR & Stress

VaR, Stressed VaR, stress tests and Expected Shortfall — the numbers risk engines produce every night, and what support does when they look wrong.

Parametric VaR: the formula approach

Value at Risk answers: "what is the most we can lose over a horizon, with a given confidence?" Parametric VaR assumes returns are normally distributed:

VaR = z × σ × √t × portfolio value

where z is 1.65 for 95% confidence (2.33 for 99%), σ is daily volatility, and t is the horizon in days.

Worked example. A $10M equity book with 1.2% daily volatility. 1-day 95% VaR = 1.65 × 0.012 × $10M = $198,000. For a 10-day horizon (the regulatory standard), scale by √10: $198,000 × 3.162 ≈ $626,000.

Interpretation: on 95 out of 100 days, the loss should not exceed $198,000. On the other 5 days, it can — and VaR says nothing about how bad those days get.

Historical VaR: let the data speak

Historical VaR makes no normality assumption. Take the last 250 daily P&L observations, sort worst to best, and read off the loss at the 5% tail — the 13th worst day (5% of 250 = 12.5). That loss is the 1-day 95% historical VaR.

It captures fat tails and real market behavior, but it is only as good as its window: a calm year produces a VaR that understates risk the moment volatility returns. That weakness is exactly why regulators added Stressed VaR.

Stressed VaR (SVaR): VaR in a crisis

SVaR runs the same VaR calculation but calibrated on a continuous 12-month period of significant market stress (2008, March 2020). Because stressed volatility is far higher, SVaR is typically 2–4× normal VaR — it answers "what would our risk look like if 2008 happened to today's book?"

Worked example. The same $10M book: normal 10-day 99% VaR = $450,000. Recalibrated on a 2008 stress window, daily vol jumps to 3.8%: SVaR = 2.33 × 0.038 × √10 × $10M ≈ $2.8M. Capital requirements are driven by the stressed number, not the calm one.

Stress testing and scenario analysis

Stress tests skip probability entirely and ask "what if?": apply a defined shock to every position and revalue. Typical scenarios: +200bp parallel rate shift, −30% global equities, oil +50%, credit spreads ×3. Unlike VaR, a stress test shows the actual dollar loss under each named scenario — no confidence level, no distribution, just the number.

Worked example. A bond portfolio with $1.2M DV01 (dollar loss per 1bp rate rise) under a +200bp shock loses roughly 200 × $1.2M = $240M before convexity. Risk committees live on these tables; when the overnight stress run fails, the desk has no official numbers at the morning meeting.

Expected Shortfall and backtesting

Expected Shortfall (ES) fixes VaR's blind spot: it is the average loss on the days VaR is breached — the tail beyond the line. Backtesting checks the model against reality: count how many times the actual loss exceeded 1-day 99% VaR over 250 days. Regulators use traffic lights: 0–4 exceptions = green, 5–9 = yellow (capital add-on), 10+ = red (model rejected).

What production support watches

  • Risk-feed breaks — VaR engines consume positions, market data and curves; a missing feed means stale or missing risk numbers, and traders notice before risk managers do.
  • Limit breaches — when VaR exceeds its limit, the alert must fire and reach the desk; a silent breach is a support incident with regulatory overtones.
  • Overnight batch failures — SVaR and stress runs are heavy jobs; a failed run at 3 AM needs triage before the 7 AM risk meeting, not after.
  • Model vs market divergence — repeated backtesting exceptions mean the model is wrong, not the market; escalate to quantitative risk, don't just restart the job.
