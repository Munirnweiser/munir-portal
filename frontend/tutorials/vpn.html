VPN

The tunnel every remote trader depends on: how VPNs work, how they fail, and the diagnostics that find it in minutes.

Site-to-site vs client VPN

A site-to-site VPN permanently connects two networks — bank data center to exchange colocation, head office to disaster-recovery site. It is always on and carries production traffic. A client (remote-access) VPN connects one laptop to the corporate network — the work-from-home trader's lifeline to the trading systems. Support cares about both, but the triage differs: a site-to-site failure hits a whole location at once; a client VPN failure hits one person and is usually their machine, their ISP, or their credentials.

How the tunnel is built: phase 1 and phase 2

IPsec VPNs negotiate in two phases. Phase 1 (IKE) establishes a secure channel between the two gateways — authenticating each side and agreeing on encryption. Phase 2 negotiates the actual data tunnel (the security associations carrying traffic). The diagnostic rule: if phase 1 fails, the gateways can't talk securely at all — check pre-shared keys, certificates, and reachability on UDP 500/4500. If phase 1 is up but phase 2 fails, check the interesting-traffic definitions (proxy IDs / traffic selectors) — the two sides disagree about what traffic the tunnel should carry.

# typical tunnel diagnostics (gateway CLI)
show crypto ikev1 sa        # phase 1 up? look for MM_ACTIVE
show crypto ipsec sa       # phase 2 up? check encaps/decaps counters
# encaps rising, decaps stuck at 0 → the far side isn't
# returning traffic: routing or selector mismatch there

The usual suspects: DPD, MTU and credentials

  • DPD (Dead Peer Detection) — heartbeats that detect a dead tunnel and rebuild it. A tunnel that "drops every 30 minutes" with no config change is often DPD misfiring across NAT, or lifetimes mismatched between sides.
  • MTU — encryption adds overhead, shrinking the effective packet size. Symptoms are maddeningly specific: small packets (chat, pings) work, large transfers (file drops, screen sharing) stall. Fix: clamp MSS or lower the tunnel MTU.
  • Credentials — expired certificates and rotated pre-shared keys kill tunnels at the worst moment; certificate expiry dates belong on a monitoring dashboard, not in someone's memory.

Incident walkthrough: trader can't reach the trading system

8:55 AM: a trader working from home reports the order-entry screen won't load; colleagues in the office are fine. Triage order: (1) Is the VPN client connected? Yes, tunnel is up. (2) Can they reach anything internal? Intranet loads — so the tunnel works and it's not credentials. (3) Traceroute to the trading server dies after the VPN gateway. The trading VLAN's route was removed from the VPN concentrator's config during weekend maintenance. Fix: re-add the route, trader reconnects, orders flow. Total: 12 minutes — because the triage isolated "tunnel up, one destination unreachable" instead of reinstalling the VPN client.

Split tunneling: convenience vs control

With full tunneling, every packet from the laptop — trading traffic and video calls alike — goes through the corporate VPN concentrator: inspectable and policy-controlled, but every byte consumes concentrator bandwidth and adds latency. With split tunneling, only corporate destinations use the tunnel; streaming and personal browsing go direct: faster for the user, cheaper for the network, but the endpoint is more exposed and DNS can leak internal names.

Banks almost always force full tunnel for trading staff — the compliance and DLP requirements leave no choice. Support angle: "the trading app is slow from home but fine in the office" on a full-tunnel setup often means the home traffic is hairpinning through an overloaded concentrator; check concentrator load and the user's assigned gateway before blaming their ISP.

Certificate renewal: where auto-enrollment breaks

Machine and user certificates typically live 1–2 years and renew via SCEP or EST auto-enrollment — silently, until it doesn't. The failure modes, in order of frequency:

  • CA unreachable — the enrollment server is down or the firewall rule changed; renewals queue up and expire together weeks later.
  • Clock skew — a laptop whose clock drifted gets a certificate that is "not yet valid"; the VPN client reports a cryptic auth failure, not a time problem.
  • Template permissions — the CA template changed and the service account can no longer enroll; every renewal fails identically.
  • Stale cached cert — the client keeps presenting the old certificate after renewal; clearing the machine cert store forces re-enrollment.

Triage order. Check the local cert store expiry dates first (certlm.msc on Windows), then the CA and event logs for SCEP errors, then time sync. The watch list at the bottom of this page already says to dashboard every expiry — this is what you do when that dashboard was never built.

SD-WAN vs classic VPN, in one paragraph

Classic VPN is static: manually configured tunnels, manual failover, all branch traffic backhauled through headquarters — simple to reason about, slow to adapt. SD-WAN builds an overlay across whatever links exist (MPLS, broadband, LTE), measures them continuously, and steers each application down the best path with sub-second failover, all from central policy. For trading sites it means better resilience and less backhaul tromboning — at the cost of a new control plane to operate: when traffic takes a surprising path, the answer is now a controller query, not a static route table, and a controller outage is a new kind of very bad day.

Latency and jitter diagnosis with mtr

mtr fuses ping and traceroute: per-hop loss and latency, updated live. Run it during the incident, for at least 50 cycles, against the real destination — and keep a baseline from a good day for comparison.

$ mtr -rwc 50 vpn-gateway.bank.example
HOST            Loss%   Avg    Best   Wrst
gateway-hop-3    0.0%   12.1   11.8   14.2
gateway-hop-4   12.0%   45.3   12.0  210.4
gateway-hop-5    0.0%   13.0   12.5   15.1

Hop 4 shows 12% loss but hop 5 is clean — the loss is that router deprioritizing ICMP, not real packet loss; real loss persists at every downstream hop. Jitter is the variance (note hop 4's worst of 210ms): voice and market-data feeds suffer from jitter long before average latency looks bad. Support rule: one mtr from the affected user beats ten theories in the bridge call.

What production support watches

  • Tunnel uptime and throughput — alert on tunnel state changes and on throughput drops, which precede user complaints by minutes.
  • Certificate expiry — track every VPN certificate's expiry centrally; a tunnel dying at 3 AM on a Sunday is always a certificate.
  • One user vs all users — the first question for any VPN ticket; it halves the troubleshooting space instantly.
  • Change correlation — VPN failures cluster around firewall and gateway maintenance windows; check the change calendar before deep diagnostics.
