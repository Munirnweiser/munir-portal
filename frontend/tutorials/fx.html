FX Essentials

Spot, forwards, swaps and NDFs — the four instruments behind almost every FX support ticket.

Spot: buying currency for immediate delivery

A spot trade exchanges two currencies with settlement two business days later (T+2; T+1 for USD/CAD and some pairs). Prices are quoted as pairs: EUR/USD 1.0850 / 1.0852. The first currency (EUR) is the base, the second (USD) is the quote. The bank buys at the bid (1.0850) and sells at the ask (1.0852); the 2-pip spread is its margin.

Worked example. A client buys EUR 1,000,000 against USD at 1.0852. The client pays USD 1,085,200 and receives EUR 1,000,000 on value date. If the trader's book was flat, the desk is now short EUR 1M / long USD 1.0852M and will hedge it in the interbank market.

A pip is the smallest quoted move — 0.0001 for most pairs, 0.01 for JPY pairs. A 50-pip move on a EUR 10M position is roughly USD 5,000 of P&L, which is why traders watch spot ladders tick by tick.

Forwards: locking in a rate for a future date

An FX forward agrees today on an exchange rate for settlement on a future date — 1 month, 3 months, 1 year out. The forward rate is not a forecast; it is mechanically derived from the spot rate and the interest-rate differential between the two currencies (covered interest parity):

F = S × (1 + rd × t) / (1 + rf × t)

where S is spot, rd the domestic rate, rf the foreign rate, and t the time in years. The difference between forward and spot is quoted in forward points (pips added to or subtracted from spot).

Worked example. USD/JPY spot is 150.00. USD 3-month rate is 5.00%, JPY 3-month rate is 0.10%, t = 90/360 = 0.25.

F = 150.00 × (1 + 0.05 × 0.25) / (1 + 0.001 × 0.25) ≈ 151.83

The forward trades about 183 pips above spot — the higher-yielding currency (USD) is at a forward discount, the lower-yielding one (JPY) at a premium. An importer needing JPY in 3 months locks in 151.83 today and has no FX uncertainty left.

Swaps: rolling positions forward

An FX swap is two trades in one ticket: a spot (or near-dated) leg and a forward leg in the opposite direction. The classic use is rolling a position — a trader long EUR/USD spot who wants to keep the position sells spot and buys the 1-week forward simultaneously. The price difference between the legs is the swap points, and the P&L on the roll reflects the interest differential earned or paid.

Support teams see swaps constantly around month-ends and IMM dates, when the whole street rolls positions at once and volumes spike.

NDFs: when the currency cannot leave the country

Some currencies (BRL, INR, KRW, CNY onshore restrictions) are not freely convertible, so forwards cannot physically settle. A non-deliverable forward settles in a convertible currency — usually USD — for the cash difference between the agreed rate and the official fixing rate on maturity.

Worked example. A fund buys a 3-month USD/BRL NDF at 5.0000 for USD 10M notional. At maturity the central-bank fixing prints 5.2000 (BRL weaker). The fund is owed (5.2000 − 5.0000) / 5.2000 × USD 10M ≈ USD 384,615, paid in USD. No reais ever change hands.

What production support watches

  • Fixing failures — NDF and corporate hedging flows depend on official fixings publishing on time; a late fixing delays valuation and settlement.
  • CLS cutoffs — spot and forward settlement runs through CLS Bank on strict deadlines; a missed pay-in creates a settlement fail and funding cost.
  • Forward-point spikes — around central-bank meetings, forward points can gap; stale pricing engines show off-market rates and traders complain within minutes.
  • Value-date validation — forwards booked on currency holidays fail downstream; calendars are reference data, and bad calendars are a classic break source.

Herstatt risk: when settlement itself is the risk

FX settlement has a unique danger: the two currency legs settle in different payment systems, hours apart. If your counterparty collapses after you have paid your leg but before they pay theirs, you lose the full principal — not just a market move. This is Herstatt risk, named after Bankhaus Herstatt, which German regulators closed on 26 June 1974 after it had received Deutsche Marks from counterparties but before its US-dollar legs settled in New York. Counterparties never saw their dollars.

The industry answer is payment-versus-payment (PvP): CLS Bank settles both legs simultaneously, so principal risk disappears — you either get both currencies or neither. That is why CLS-eligible trades are the safe path, and why non-CLS settlement (many emerging-market pairs) still carries genuine principal risk that support teams must monitor trade by trade.

CLS pay-in schedule: a worked funding example

CLS nets each member's trades per currency, then issues a pay-in schedule: the net amount you must deliver, in each currency, by its deadline (roughly 07:00–12:00 CET depending on the currency — JPY early, USD later in the morning). Miss a pay-in and your trades do not settle; you also pay funding costs on the shortfall.

Worked example. A bank's CLS book for value date shows: pay EUR 50M, receive EUR 20M → net pay-in EUR 30M by the EUR deadline; pay USD 10M, receive USD 60M → net receive USD 50M (no pay-in needed). Treasury funds only the net EUR 30M — netting is the whole point — but if the EUR 30M arrives 20 minutes late, the EUR settlements fail and the bank pays CLS fail funding charges for the day.

Support watches: the pay-in monitor dashboard on value date, SWIFT confirmation of each pay-in (MT202/MT103 flows), and any currency where the funding desk has not confirmed cover 30 minutes before deadline.

FX swap points: a worked calculation

Swap points are just the forward premium expressed in pips. From covered interest parity, with spot S, domestic (quote-currency) rate rd, foreign (base-currency) rate rf, and t in years:

Swap points = (F − S) × 10,000   where   F = S × (1 + rd×t) / (1 + rf×t)

Worked example. EUR/USD spot 1.0850. EUR 3-month rate 3.00%, USD 3-month rate 5.00%, t = 90/360 = 0.25.

F = 1.0850 × (1 + 0.05×0.25) / (1 + 0.03×0.25) = 1.0850 × 1.0125 / 1.0075 ≈ 1.0904

Swap points = (1.0904 − 1.0850) × 10,000 ≈ +54 pips. The higher-yielding dollar sits at a forward discount, so rolling a long EUR/USD position forward earns 54 pips per 3 months — and a pricing engine quoting +40 or +70 is visibly off-market, which is exactly the kind of ticket support investigates.
